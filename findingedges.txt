1) Required Inputs
To function correctly, the framework requires specific data inputs before any testing begins:
• Granular Historical Data: Time-series data including Open, High, Low, Close, and Volume. Data must include delisted assets to prevent survivorship bias.
• Liquidity and Cost Models: Historical bid/ask spread data and a predefined commission structure (flat fee or percentage).
• Execution Constraints: Execution Constraints:

For robustness testing, slippage is applied as 1 tick on entry + 1 tick on exit (2 ticks round-trip), independent of baseline cost assumptions.
This stress test is mandatory and is used to invalidate fragile strategies.
• The "Universe" of Trials: A complete record of every strategy variation, parameter set, and rule tested—not just the final selection. This is required to calculate the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) and correct for selection bias.
• Risk Parameters: Defined risk limits (e.g., maximum drawdown, position sizing) and investor risk aversion levels (turbulence index).
2) Mandatory Steps (Strict Order)
The backtesting process must follow this chronological and methodological sequence to maintain statistical integrity:
1. Hypothesis and Plan Formulation: Before coding, establish a written plan defining entry/exit logic, risk management rules, and the specific market conditions (regimes) the strategy targets. This prevents "data snooping" or retrofitting logic to data.
2. Data Partitioning (Purging & Embargoing): Split data into Training (In-Sample), Validation (In-Time Out-of-Sample), and Testing (Blind Out-of-Sample) sets.
    ◦ Purging: Remove observations from the training set that overlap with the labels (outcomes) of the test set to prevent information leakage.
    ◦ Embargoing: Delete a section of data immediately following the test set to prevent leakage from serial correlation.
3. Event-Driven Simulation: Execute the strategy using an event-driven loop (processing data bar-by-bar or tick-by-tick) rather than vectorized calculations. This enforces strict time adherence, ensuring the system only acts on data available at the exact moment of the decision.
4. Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO): Instead of a single static test, use a rolling-window approach. Train parameters on a past window, test on the immediate future segment, and roll the window forward. This tests the strategy's ability to adapt to non-stationary data (changing market regimes).
5. Combinatorial Cross-Validation: Generate multiple backtest paths by splitting data into N groups and testing on all combinations of k groups (Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation). This generates a distribution of results rather than a single historical path, simulating various market scenarios.
6. Monte Carlo Permutation (The "Randomness" Test): Randomly shuffle the pairing of positions (entries/exits) and market returns thousands of times to create a "null distribution" of a worthless strategy. Compare the candidate strategy's performance against this distribution to ensure results are not due to luck.
7. Paper Trading (Real-Time Simulation): Run the strategy in a live environment with real-time data (without capital) to verify that execution logic (latency, API connectivity) matches the simulation.
3) Required Performance Metrics
Do not rely solely on Net Profit. The following metrics are required to define the "Edge" and robustness:
• Expectancy (in R-Multiples): Calculated as (Win Rate × Average Win) - (Loss Rate × Average Loss). This standardizes performance across different assets and volatilities.
• Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR): A variance-adjusted Sharpe ratio that corrects for the number of trials attempted (selection bias) and the non-normality (skewness/kurtosis) of returns.
• Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR): The probability that the observed Sharpe ratio exceeds a given threshold (e.g., 0) with a specific confidence level (e.g., 95%).
• Calmar Ratio: The ratio of annualized return to Maximum Drawdown, used to assess return relative to the risk of ruin.
• Probability of Overfitting (PBO): A metric derived from combinatorial cross-validation that estimates the likelihood the selected strategy is overfitted to specific data.

Based on the provided sources, a genuine trading edge is defined not merely by past profitability, but by specific statistical validity, the ability to withstand realistic cost models, and the rejection of the null hypothesis (random luck) after accounting for the number of attempts made to find the strategy.
Here is the explicit definition of a trading edge categorized by your requirements:
1. Statistical Properties of an Edge
To qualify as a genuine edge, a strategy must exhibit specific mathematical characteristics that differentiate it from random noise:
• Positive Expectancy: The most fundamental property is a positive expectancy, often expressed in "R-multiples" (units of risk). It is calculated as (Win Rate × Average Win) - (Loss Rate × Average Loss). An edge exists when the winners weighted by their frequency exceed the losers weighted by their frequency.
• Statistically Significant Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR): A high Sharpe ratio (SR) alone is insufficient due to non-normal returns and selection bias. A genuine edge must possess a significant Deflated Sharpe Ratio, which adjusts the SR for the number of trials attempted (selection bias) and the skewness/kurtosis of returns. It must reject the null hypothesis that the strategy is worthless with a high confidence level (e.g., 95%).
• Rejection of Randomness (Monte Carlo Permutation): An edge is confirmed if the strategy’s return falls in the extreme tail of a null distribution generated by randomly shuffling the pairings of positions and market returns. If a strategy’s performance can be easily replicated by random permutations, it lacks a genuine edge.
• Predictive Power Beyond Market Bias: In a biased market (e.g., a bull market), a long-only strategy might show profit without skill. A genuine edge persists after "centering" the returns (removing the average market drift) to isolate the strategy's ability to beat the odds.
2. Minimum Sample Conditions
An edge cannot be confirmed without sufficient data to rule out luck:
• Minimum Trade Count: Testing with small samples (e.g., 20–30 trades) renders results unreliable. A minimum of 100 trades is suggested to achieve basic statistical reliability, with hundreds being ideal to cover various market conditions.
• Minimum Track Record Length (MinTRL): There is a mathematically calculated minimum time required to distinguish a genuine edge from a lucky streak. MinTRL increases if the returns have negative skewness (fat tails) or if the observed Sharpe ratio is close to the threshold. For example, an observed SR of 0.95 might require approximately 3 years of data to be statistically significant.

3. Invalidators of an Edge (Even if Profitable)
A strategy may show a profit in a backtest but still possess no genuine edge if any of the following are present:
• Intolerance to Friction: A strategy has no edge if it becomes unprofitable after factoring in realistic transaction costs, including commissions and, crucially, slippage. If a single tick of slippage destroys the profit, the edge is likely illusory.
• Look-Ahead Bias: An edge is invalid if the simulation used data not available at the moment of the decision (e.g., using closing prices to execute trades earlier in the day). This "leakage" inflates performance metrics.
• Survivorship Bias: Testing only on assets that currently exist (ignoring delisted or bankrupt assets) creates a false edge by artificially removing losers from the sample.
• Lack of Embargoing: In time-series analysis, an edge is invalidated if training data overlaps with test data. Proper validation requires "purging" overlapping observations and "embargoing" data immediately following a test set to prevent leakage from serial correlation.
4. Overfitting vs. Robustness
Distinguishing between a model that "memorized" history and one that found a genuine pattern is critical:
• Definition of Overfitting: Overfitting (or curve-fitting) occurs when a strategy is tailored too closely to past noise rather than signal. It is characterized as a "high-variance" model: it fits the training data perfectly but degrades substantially when applied to out-of-sample data. Indicators of overfitting include strategies that rely on "lucky" outliers or specific market regimes that are unlikely to repeat.
• Probability of Overfitting (PBO): This is a quantitative metric derived from combinatorial cross-validation. A low PBO (e.g., < 5%) suggests the strategy is not overfitted, whereas a high PBO indicates the results are likely a fluke.
• Robustness: Robustness is the ability of a strategy to perform consistently across different market environments (regimes) and timeframes.
    ◦ Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO): Robustness is tested by optimizing on a rolling window of past data and testing on a subsequent "out-of-sample" window. A robust strategy maintains performance in the forward-looking window.
    ◦ Parameter Stability: A robust edge should not depend on a "knife-edge" parameter (e.g., a specific moving average length). If small changes to parameters cause performance to collapse, the edge is overfitted.

1. Selection Bias (Multiple Testing Bias)
• Why it looks valid: The engine tests thousands of strategy variations and eventually finds one with a high Sharpe ratio (e.g., > 2.0). The result appears statistically significant because it outperforms the market baseline on historical data,.
• Why it is wrong: According to the False Strategy Theorem, the expected maximum Sharpe ratio increases purely as a function of the number of trials (N). If an engine runs enough tests, it is mathematically guaranteed to find a strategy that looks profitable by random chance, even on noise,. The "best" strategy is often just the "luckiest" one, not the most skillful.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Log All Trials: The system must record every strategy configuration and parameter set tested, not just the winners.
    ◦ Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR): Apply the DSR formula, which adjusts the Sharpe ratio threshold based on the total number of independent trials (N) and the non-normality of returns (skewness/kurtosis). Reject any strategy that does not pass the DSR confidence level (e.g., 95%),.
    ◦ Split Data: Utilize a true "blind" hold-out set that is never used during the selection process.
2. Look-Ahead Bias (Data Leakage)
• Why it looks valid: The strategy exhibits exceptional predictive accuracy and a smooth equity curve. Standard machine learning cross-validation (like random k-fold) often produces these robust metrics,.
• Why it is wrong: The model inadvertently used information from the future to make decisions in the past. Common causes include:
    ◦ Feature Engineering: Calculating indicators (e.g., Z-scores or normalization) using the mean/standard deviation of the entire dataset rather than a rolling window,.
    ◦ Shuffling: Randomly shuffling time-series data for cross-validation destroys chronological order, allowing the model to train on future data points,.
    ◦ Timestamp Mismatch: Using closing prices to execute trades assumed to occur earlier in the day.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Purging and Embargoing: When splitting data, "purge" training observations that overlap with the label computation of the test set. Add an "embargo" (buffer period) after the test set to prevent leakage from serial correlation,.
    ◦ Event-Driven Architecture: Use an event-driven backtester that processes data tick-by-tick or bar-by-bar, strictly forbidding access to future array indices,.
    ◦ Rolling Statistics: Enforce that all feature transformations are calculated strictly on data available up to the current moment.
3. Overfitting (Curve-Fitting)
• Why it looks valid: The strategy fits the historical data perfectly, capturing every major market turn and showing high returns with low drawdown. It may have complex rules or many parameters that seem to "solve" the market.
• Why it is wrong: The model has "memorized" the noise of a specific historical dataset rather than learning a generalizable economic signal. These strategies fail immediately in live markets because the specific pattern of noise does not repeat (non-stationarity),.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO): Instead of a static split, train on a window, test on the following period, and roll this window forward. A robust strategy must perform consistently across these rolling out-of-sample periods,.
    ◦ Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (CPCV): Generate a distribution of Sharpe ratios from many different backtest paths (combinations of groups) to ensure the result isn't dependent on a single specific historical path,.
    ◦ Probability of Overfitting (PBO): Calculate the PBO metric; if high, the strategy is likely fitting noise.
4. Unrealistic Execution Assumptions
• Why it looks valid: The backtest shows steady profit accumulation, often with a high win rate on small price moves,.
• Why it is wrong: It ignores slippage, spreads, and market impact.
    ◦ Strategies that scalp small profits (e.g., 1-2 ticks) often turn into losers once the bid-ask spread and commissions are factored in.
    ◦ Assuming execution at the "Close" or "Open" price without accounting for liquidity or latency is a major source of error,.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Transaction Cost Modeling: Include a cost model that simulates commissions and, crucially, slippage (e.g., random or volatility-scaled slippage),.
    ◦ Worst-Case Testing: Assume the trade always executes at the worst possible price (e.g., across the spread) to see if the edge survives.
    ◦ Latency Simulation: Insert a time delay (e.g., a few seconds) between signal generation and trade execution.
5. Survivorship Bias
• Why it looks valid: The engine backtests against a dataset of currently major assets (e.g., "Current S&P 500"), showing consistent growth and stability.
• Why it is wrong: The dataset artificially excludes companies that went bankrupt or were delisted during the historical period. The engine is testing on a pre-selected list of "winners," creating a massive upward bias that does not exist in real-time trading,.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Delisted Data: The research environment must include data for assets that have been delisted or went bankrupt.
    ◦ Point-in-Time Universes: Select the tradable universe based only on the list of assets that were available/listed on that specific historical date.
6. Ignoring Non-Stationarity (Regime Changes)
• Why it looks valid: A model trained on a specific period (e.g., a long bull market) shows high predictive accuracy and profitability.
• Why it is wrong: Financial data is non-stationary; statistical properties (mean, variance) change over time. A strategy optimized for low volatility will likely collapse during a high-volatility crisis,.
• How a system should detect and prevent it:
    ◦ Regime-Based Testing: Evaluate the strategy across different defined market regimes (bull, bear, high vol, low vol) to ensure robustness.
    ◦ Structural Break Detection: The system should monitor for structural breaks in time series and trigger retraining or halting if the market environment shifts significantly from the training data.
